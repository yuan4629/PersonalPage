EditJudgeBias: a preservation-gated counterfactual audit of MLLM judges

Sole first author Conception through writing In preparation · targeting ICLR 2027

This is not a paper yet. It is the work as it stands: what the study does, what it found, and the four conclusions of my own that the audit overturned.

🎯 The problem

Leaderboards for instruction-based image editing increasingly rank models using multimodal LLM judges. Those judges are not themselves audited. The question here is how much of a judge's score comes from edit quality, and how much from surface cues that have nothing to do with it.

The methodological commitment is narrow and load-bearing: only admit a perturbation you can show did not change edit quality. Preservation verification is not a post-hoc sanity check here — it is an admission gate every injector and every metric has to pass first, with coverage reported layer by layer. Each cue also carries a zero-dose sham control, which establishes a floor per judge and per metric; without that floor a null result is unreadable. Preservation checking itself is not new. Making it a gate, and pairing it with sham floors, is the departure.

Cues span four injection sites — protocol level (order only), pixel level, content level, and prompt level — plus a null-perturbation control. Base images come from five public sources, passed through a text / sha1 / ORB near-duplicate gate to yield three mutually independent pools. The replication arm carries its own Benjamini–Hochberg family and is never pooled with the main judge list.

The κ above is printed with its ceiling because it sits on it: κmax = 0.8930 for this label distribution, so the figure reports a prevalence structure, not annotator skill. No statistic in this project is quoted alone.

🔍 Findings

  1. Protocol-level reordering alone flips pairwise decisions at 38.2× the judge's self-noise.
  2. Five pixel- and content-level cues depress scores consistently across 5/5 judges; a bandwagon cue is the only one that consistently inflates across 5/5.
  3. Only one cue holds up across both judges and content pools. Most of the rest are order-preserving depressions: they move the absolute score without moving the ranking.
  4. Rubric-style scoring buys absolute-score stability but not rank stability — a counterexample to the common assumption that a rubric makes a judge more reliable.
  5. Of three mitigation layers only the protocol-level one works, and follow-up analysis shows its mechanism is abstention, not debiasing. Prompt-level mitigation is near coin-flip; ensemble median is worse than doing nothing.
  6. A 5/5 null result on a skin-tone attribute — readable only because same-batch positive controls fired. Testing specificity alone would certify a judge that never alarms as the best-calibrated one: revocation sensitivity was 0.032 for one judge and 0.946 for another on identical data.

🧪 Whether to believe it

I spent comparable effort on whether to believe my own results. The repository carries 1,307 unit tests and 109 frozen artifacts, each with a sha256, its producer, and a field recording what that table must not be read as. There is a single reconstruction path with byte-level comparison after a full rebuild, and the anonymized delivery package re-verifies itself: 1,289 tests pass inside the package, and 112 analysis tables rebuilt from raw judgments come out byte-identical to the repository.

Four times the audit overturned a conclusion I had already written down:

Clustering not applied
Claim B's significant cells corrected from 13/40 down to 2/40.
Mechanism misread
"Protocol-level mitigation debiases the judge" reclassified as abstention.
Control was missing
"This cue is the only one breaking preservation" refuted by my own added control.
Cost accounting
Dashboard-delta method understated spend by ~19%; replaced with a per-call ledger.

Selecting a human-anchor dataset surfaced two contamination modes: one candidate shared 70% of its base images with a dataset already in use, and another's advertised human scores turned out to be machine scores from an automatic metric — adopting it would have made the argument circular. These and other cases are collected into a running list of 27 silent analysis-layer failure modes, each of which had produced a publication-ready wrong number in this project.

Related but not the same question: representation-level accounts of judge bias (Xu et al., arXiv:2607.11871) ask what happens inside a text judge when it scores unfairly. This asks whether a multimodal judge's score can be trusted as a measurement at all, and supplies the admission gate and null-readability machinery needed to answer that.

🧭 Where this goes

My three projects are the same question asked three times.

Geolocation A correct answer is not a correct reasoning process.
Social deduction Persuasive language is not strategic correctness.
Multimodal judging A verdict that looks right is not a verdict that used the right evidence.

Each time, surface competence and underlying competence come apart — and our instruments mostly measure the first. That is the question I expect to spend years on: why can a language model be this fluent and this capable at the surface, and still lack robust reasoning, strategy, and depth? What is missing between generating language and thinking? I want to work on it at three levels.

  1. Measure the gap. Evaluation grounded in real human behavior and real cognitive process, designed to separate surface success from the capacity it stands in for. This is what the work so far has been.
  2. Explain it. Mechanistic accounts of what inside the model produces the failure, rather than one more report that it fails.
  3. Close it. Interventions that change the underlying process instead of the benchmark number. A gain that leaves the process untouched is the failure mode here, not the goal.

Further out: what computational process turns language generation into reasoning, conceptual restructuring, and discovery? The aim is not a model that writes more like someone who thinks. It is a science of machine thought, and a smaller gap between fluency and thought.

🌱 Also under way

PromptChecker. Instructions arrive one at a time, and an instruction is usually not enough to specify the problem. A model that answers immediately is answering a question that was never fully asked. The direction: reconstruct what the user actually needs before responding, and treat "I do not yet have enough to act" as a first-class output rather than a failure to comply.
